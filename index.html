Method and benchmark

At recruitment events, both sides now speak through AI.

Polished CVs meet AI screeners, and nobody learns what anyone can actually do. Common Ground runs the event on real work instead: companies say what they need done, people show what they have done in their own words, and a stable matching builds the day.

The event is fine. The information going into it is not.

Organisers in Espoo fill the room. What they cannot see is who should talk to whom, because both sides describe themselves badly, late, and increasingly through a model.

Companies
send a job title a week before the day. “A full-stack dev” says nothing about the work waiting on Monday.
People
with transferable skills cannot get them past a keyword filter. A café manager who cut waste by a third is not a “data analyst”.
Organisers
get hundreds of machine-written applications and no way to tell real capability from fluent text.

How it works

Four steps, each one a screen in the workspace. Nothing below is a mock-up: the examples are from the Espoo event data.

1

A company’s need becomes three concrete tasks

A company types what it would say to a colleague. Three short questions turn that into three tasks someone could start on Monday, each tied to skills from ESCO, the European skills classification. The company edits and sends them.

a developer for internal tools

  • Build an internal web tool to track support requests.about 13 people
  • Find and fix bugs in the customer portal before release.about 13 people
  • Write a short guide for the team using the new tool.about 36 people

The count is people at this event with proof for that work.

2

Work stories become skills, each with a proof line

People write three short stories about work they did. No CV, no titles. A model suggests ESCO skills, but a skill only counts if an exact sentence the person wrote proves it. The person sees every skill and its sentence, changes or removes any of them, and approves the card.

I built a spreadsheet that logged everything we threw away, by day and by reason. I compared the totals each week and found that most waste came from sandwiches prepared after lunch.

perform data analysisuse spreadsheets software

Companies see the sentence, without a name, until both sides say yes.

3

A stable matching builds the day

People ask for the tables they prefer; each table holds the people with the most proof for its tasks. This is deferred acceptance, the method behind the doctor–hospital match. Meetings are then fitted into ten-minute slots, and an independent check counts blocking pairs: a person and a company who would both rather meet each other than the meetings they got.

4

Everyone gets a day page that works offline

The organiser sends proposals; nothing is booked until both sides say yes. Each person and company then gets one plain page: time, table, why you two, and a first question. It is a static document, saved on the phone, so it opens when the venue wifi does not and when everyone logs in at once.

Proof, including where it loses

One synthetic event of 120 people and 15 companies, scored against hidden labels that were frozen before any matching. Held-out split, 20 scheduling runs per method.

What Common Ground offers a city is a schedule that is stable, explainable sentence by sentence, and blind to names, nationality, language and school. These are scenario fixtures with labels we wrote, not field results. Read the full method, the benchmark and its limits.

What it never does

  • It never ranks people for a job or rejects anyone. It proposes ten-minute meetings. The organiser sends them and both sides answer each one.
  • It never reads a name, nationality, language, school, age or photo. Group data is kept in a separate file and used only to count who gets meetings.
  • It never shows a company a name before both sides say yes. Until then a company sees one sentence of proof.
  • It never keeps a CV. Stories and the evidence card can be deleted by the person at any time.

Recruitment software may be high-risk under Annex III of the EU AI Act. This prototype has not been assessed against it; the controls above support human oversight and do not establish compliance.
